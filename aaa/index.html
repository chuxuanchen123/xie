Yangtze giant softshell turtle

A creature doomed to perish

1.General Information

Name of the species:

Yangtze giant softshell turtle (also called Swinhoe's softshell turtle); in Chinese, 斑鳖 (bān biē).

Where does this species live? (List countries, regions, or habitats)

Historically it lived in the middle and lower Yangtze River basin, the Qiantang River basin and the Taihu Lake region in China, and in the Red River (Honghe) basin in southern China and northern Vietnam. Today no breeding population is known in the wild: the only confirmed individuals are one male at Suzhou Shangfangshan Forest Zoo in Jiangsu, China, and one male in Dong Mo Lake near Hanoi, Vietnam.


2.Reasons for Extinction

What are the main threats to this species?

The main threats are: (1) hunting and poaching for meat and traditional medicine, which caused a sharp decline in the 1990s; (2) habitat loss and change — dams and hydroelectric barrages, sand mining that destroys nesting beaches, wetland drainage, and water pollution; (3) bycatch in fishing gear; and (4) the extremely small population itself, which makes genetic diversity and successful reproduction almost impossible.

When did it become endangered?

It was assessed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List in 2000, and it is a first-class nationally protected animal in China. By then hunting and habitat loss had already reduced it to a handful of animals: the last confirmed sighting in the wild in China was in 1998, in Yunnan Province. The last known female died at Suzhou Zoo in April 2019 during an artificial-insemination procedure.


3.Description of the Species

What does this species look like? (Describe size, color, special features)

It is one of the largest freshwater turtles in the world: the shell can grow more than 1 metre long and the body can weigh up to about 100 kg. Its carapace is leathery, grey-green and covered with yellow spots — which is why it is called Yangtze Giant Softshell Turtle, “spotted softshell”. It has a long, pig-like snout, eyes set on top of its head, distinctive stripes on the head and neck, and fully webbed feet.

What does it eat?

It is carnivorous. It eats fish, crabs, shrimps, snails, frogs, aquatic insects and carrion, and usually ambushes its prey and sucks it into its mouth with a powerful throat movement.


4.Conservation Efforts

Are there any projects or organizations working to save this species?

Yes. Suzhou Shangfangshan Forest Zoo keeps the last known Chinese male and has run a breeding programme since 2008, including natural mating and artificial insemination. In Vietnam, the Asian Turtle Program (ATP) monitors Dong Mo Lake and works with local authorities to protect the turtle and its habitat. The IUCN Tortoise and Freshwater Turtle Specialist Group coordinates research, scientists are using environmental DNA (eDNA) to search for surviving animals, and in 2025 the Endangered Species Fund offered a reward of 100,000 yuan for a confirmed sighting. Researchers have also discussed cloning.

What actions can people take to help?

Never buy, eat or keep wild turtles, and do not try to catch a suspected turtle yourself — report it to a conservation group instead. Protect rivers, lakes and wetlands, save water and reduce pollution, and support stricter control of sand mining and illegal fishing. People can also donate to or volunteer with conservation organisations and tell others about the species, because public awareness is what keeps conservation work funded.


5.Interesting Facts

Share two interesting or surprising facts about this species:

(1) It is a “living fossil”: softshell turtles have existed since the age of the dinosaurs, and individuals can live more than 100 years — the Suzhou male is estimated to be over 100 years old.

(2) In China it was long mistaken for the similar 鼋 (Pelochelys cantorii); only DNA tests in 2002–2005 confirmed that the giant softshell turtles kept in Suzhou and Shanghai were actually Rafetus swinhoei.
